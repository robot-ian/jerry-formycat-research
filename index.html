Prediction only helps a predator that can turn.

Three days of parameter sweeps on a 2D pursuit simulation. The cat's look-ahead distance has no good value on its own — every apparent optimum moves when you change how fast the cat can steer. 18,000 episodes.

What the numbers say

Finding 01 — measurement

A single episode is a coin flip, not a result.

The first sweep ran one episode per setting from a fixed start and recorded LEAD 1.5 as "mouse survives." Re-run with randomised starts over 500 episodes, the same setting catches the mouse 332 times out of 500.

Repeating LEAD 3.0 at 50 episodes gave 63.3s, 79.7s, 87.0s, 79.7s — a 24-second spread on an unchanged setting. At 500 episodes the standard deviation still sits near 40s, so differences under about 5s remain noise.

Finding 02 — mechanism

Lead pursuit fails against prey that turns at walls.

Against the instant-turn cat, catch rate climbs to 428/500 at LEAD 2.0, then collapses to 130/500 across LEAD 2.5–3.5 before recovering at 5.0.

Watching the animation explains it: the cat commits to an intercept point that assumes the mouse holds its heading. The wall-slide policy breaks that assumption exactly when the cat is closest. Below 2.0 the prediction is too short for the error to matter; above 5.0 the aim point is so distant the cat is effectively back to chasing a direction.

Finding 03 — coupling

Changing the cat's body changes its best strategy.

At TURN 0.15 the same LEAD 2.5–3.5 band drops to 14–54 catches and never recovers — a heavy cat cannot undo a bad commitment. At TURN 0.50 that band becomes the cat's strongest region, peaking at 389/500, and LEAD 5.0 catches the mouse in all 500 episodes.

The best look-ahead is 2.0 for an instant cat, 1.5 for a heavy one, and 5.0 for an agile one. Tuning either parameter alone finds a local optimum that dissolves as soon as the other moves.

Finding 04 — policy design

Greedy rules cannot escape a corner.

A mouse that always moves directly away from the cat pins itself at (10, 10) and is caught in 6.25s despite being 50% faster. Adding a wall-avoidance force to the flee force produces oscillation — the two cancel. Switching between discrete modes instead of blending them resolves it.

Escaping a real corner requires moving toward the cat to get past it: worse now, better in two seconds. No rule that maximises the current step will ever choose that. The reward in Phase 3 has to be survival, not distance.

Method

Each day changed one thing and measured it.

Day 103 Aug

Baseline: flee directly away

2D box, cat moves at the mouse, mouse moves directly away. Deterministic, one fixed start.

mouse_vel = (mouse − cat) / ‖mouse − cat‖
Day 204 Aug

Wall-aware policy

Detect nearby walls, rotate that direction 90°, pick the slide direction whose dot product with the flee vector is larger. Mode switch, not force blend.

slide = [−push[1], push[0]] · argmax dot(slide, flee)
Day 305 Aug

Lead pursuit and a headless evaluator

The cat aims ahead of the mouse. Start positions randomised with a 3.0-unit minimum separation. New batch runner reports mean, standard deviation, and catch rate over N episodes.

to_mouse = (mouse + mouse_vel · MOUSE_SPEED · lead) − cat
Day 416 Aug

Cat momentum

The cat carries a velocity and steers toward its desired heading rather than snapping to it. Sweeping both parameters turns the earlier curve into a surface.

cat_vel += (desired − cat_vel) · TURN_RATE
